Borrowing a deep network

GOVT 6039 · Section 10 · 18 November 2026 · on Lectures 18 and 19

Lecture 19's network read handwritten digits at 72% after 21,844 labelled examples. Speech is harder than digits, and you will have a few hundred coded sentences, not twenty thousand. So we borrow a network someone else trained: XLM-R, 24 layers, trained further on 1.72 billion words from 26 European parliaments, then fitted to sentences coded for tone. When can we trust it?

The question

Political scientists often want to know the tone of what politicians say: does the opposition attack more than the government, do MPs get harsher before an election? Coding thousands of sentences by hand is slow. Today we ask whether a network trained by someone else can do the coding, and how to check when its numbers can be trusted.

The data: ParlaSent

ParlaSent (Mochtak, Rupnik and Ljubešić 2023) is a collection of single sentences from parliamentary debates. Trained coders read each sentence and gave it one of six codes for tone, which we write as numbers from 0 to 5. Each row also records who said it and when: country, party, gender, date, and whether the party was in government. Five rows from the files we use:

The data come in seven files of 2,600 sentences. In the five training files two coders coded every sentence and then agreed on a final code; network A below learned from these. The two networks

Both start from XLM-R, a network with 24 layers and about 560 million weights. It learned language by a fill-in-the-blank game on text in 100 languages: hide a word, predict it. Researchers then trained it further on speeches from 26 European parliaments. From there the two networks part ways.

How many weights?

1.1Lecture 19 showed how nnet trained the turnout network: quasi-Newton updates θ(t+1) = θ(t) − H−1s, which need the curvature matrix H. Could the same method train this network?

__CHEV__Answer
No. H holds one number per pair of weights: about 1012 for the last two layers alone, 3 × 1017 for the whole network. It was trained the way Lecture 19 trained its wide network, with SGD on minibatches and one slope per weight from backpropagation (4 epochs, nb = 32, α = 8 × 10−6).

1.2Play “UK · a miss”. Coders use whole numbers from 0 to 5. Why can ŷ be 3.3, or rise above 5?

__CHEV__Answer
The output layer is a regression on the derived features (linout = TRUE in nnet), so nothing ties ŷ to whole numbers or to the range of the scale. Here the coder read a plea for fairness as positive (5); the network was unsure (3.3). To compare groups, leave ŷ as it is; to report single scores, clip it to [0, 5].

5Does the opposition sound more negative?

A measure is only as good as the comparison you make with it. First, check the covariate: whether each UK speaker's party was in government, as the data's ruling field records it.

5.1What went wrong in the data?

__CHEV__Answer
The Conservatives governed throughout: with the Liberal Democrats until May 2015, alone after that. The field records coalition membership, so a single-party government counts as not governing. Recode government status from party and date. Nothing in the network's accuracy would have warned you.

What to take away

  1. Lecture 18 sits on top. The last two layers are a one-hidden-layer network with p = J = 1,024; the 24 layers below supply the basis functions we cannot hand-build for words.
  2. Score it where it was not trained. Network A had seen Czech codes, so Czech went to network B. Always ask what a borrowed network saw.
  3. Borrow the closest network. A network fine-tuned for your task beats frozen features plus your own codes, which beat word counts.
  4. Judge it by your comparison. Sentence-level R² is not enough: check that the errors are the same in the groups you compare.
